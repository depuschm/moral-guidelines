# Christianity

Christianity is the faith of those who follow Jesus of Nazareth and confess him as the Christ. Its scripture is the Bible: the Old Testament, also known as the Hebrew Scriptures, and the New Testament, which tells of Jesus' life and teaching and of the early churches ([Bible Gateway, NRSVUE](https://www.biblegateway.com/versions/New-Revised-Standard-Version-Updated-Edition-NRSVUE-Bible/)). Its main traditions are the Catholic Church, the Eastern and Oriental Orthodox churches, the Protestant churches and the Pentecostal movement ([SAET, Christian Ethics: Christian ethics in diverse historical and ecumenical perspectives](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/ChristianEthics#section4)).

Jesus summed up the Law in two commandments, to love God and to love one's neighbour ([Matthew 22:34–40](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2022%3A34-40&version=NRSVUE)), and Paul wrote that love is "the fulfilling of the law" ([Romans 13:8–10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans%2013%3A8-10&version=NRSVUE)). Christian moral teaching draws on scripture, on the example of Jesus, on the teaching of the churches and, in many traditions, on reason and conscience. The churches teach that it is God's grace that makes Christians able to live this life ([Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1691–1698](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P5D.HTM), [SAET, Christian Ethics: An ethic of grace](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/ChristianEthics#section4.3)).

The churches share these sources but weigh them differently: the Catholic Church gives a central place to natural law and to its teaching office, the Orthodox churches to growth in the likeness of God, and the Protestant churches to scripture and the individual conscience.

## Differences within the tradition

- `7` [Catechism, 2032–2040](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P74.HTM) and [SEP, The Natural Law Tradition in Ethics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/natural-law-ethics/): the **Catholic Church** grounds much of its moral teaching in natural law, which it holds can be known by reason, and teaches that the authority of its teaching office (the Magisterium) extends to the precepts of natural law. Its teaching on some questions of sexual and family ethics, such as contraception, abortion, marriage and divorce, remains largely closed to discussion, although many Catholics dispute parts of it ([SAET, Christian Ethics: A Roman Catholic perspective](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/ChristianEthics#section4.1)).
- `7` [SAET, Christian Ethics: Eastern Orthodox perspectives](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/ChristianEthics#section4.2): the **Orthodox churches** see the aim of the moral life as deification (*theosis*), growing from the image into the likeness of God by grace, through the practice of the virtues, prayer and asceticism, and extend it to the transfiguration of all creation, which grounds the environmental teaching of Ecumenical Patriarch Bartholomew.
- `7` [Westminster Confession of Faith, chapters 1 and 20](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html) and [SAET, Christian Ethics: Lutheran and Pentecostal perspectives](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/ChristianEthics#section4.3): the **Protestant churches** hold scripture to be the rule of faith and life. Luther taught that Christians are justified by grace alone, and good works follow as the fruit of faith; Reformed confessions teach that "God alone is Lord of the conscience", free from human commandments contrary to scripture. Protestant churches range widely on questions such as gender and sexuality, and Pentecostal churches, for example, include both those that limit leadership to men and those moving to more egalitarian practice.
- `7` [Catechism, 2302–2317](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P81.HTM) and [Mennonite Church USA, Confession of Faith, Article 22](https://www.mennoniteusa.org/who-are-mennonites/what-we-believe/confession-of-faith/peace-justice-and-nonresistance/): on **war**, Catholic teaching, drawing on a just war tradition that goes back to Augustine and Aquinas, allows military force for defence under strict conditions, while the Mennonite and Quaker traditions practise nonresistance and refuse to take part in war ([SAET, Christian Ethics: Just war reasoning?](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/ChristianEthics#section7.2), [Quaker Faith & Practice, 24.04](https://qfp.quaker.org.uk/passage/24-04/)).
