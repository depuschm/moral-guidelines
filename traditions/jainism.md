# Jainism

Jainism is one of the religious traditions of the Indian subcontinent. Jain tradition holds that its teachings are eternal, without a founder; in the present age they go back to Mahavira, the 24th Jina or teacher in a line of 24, who lived in the sixth century BCE and was a contemporary of the Buddha ([IEP, Jain Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/jain/), [Jainpedia, Vows](https://jainpedia.org/article/themes/principles/jain-beliefs/vows/)). Most Jains live in India, and there are Jain communities in many other countries ([Jainpedia, Sects](https://jainpedia.org/article/themes/principles/sects/)).

The two main traditions, the Svetambaras and the Digambaras, split early in the Common Era and recognise different canons of scripture, but both accept the *Tattvartha Sutra* of Umasvati (Umasvami to the Digambaras) as authoritative ([Jainpedia, Sects](https://jainpedia.org/article/themes/principles/sects/), [Jainpedia, Tattvārtha-sūtra](https://jainpedia.org/article/themes/principles/sacred-writings/tattvartha-sutra/)). Lay Jains often see themselves as followers of a particular order of monks and nuns ([Jainpedia, Sects](https://jainpedia.org/article/themes/principles/sects/)).

Jain ethics is grounded in its account of the soul (*jiva*). Every living being, down to the smallest, has a soul; souls are bound in the cycle of rebirth by karma, which Jains understand as a kind of matter that actions and passions attract to the soul, and the aim of life is to free the soul by stopping and wearing away this karma. Deliberate violence against other beings produces the worst karma, which is why non-violence stands at the centre of Jain ethics. Jainism denies a creator God who controls the universe ([IEP, Jain Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/jain/), [Jainpedia, Non-violence](https://jainpedia.org/article/themes/principles/jain-beliefs/non-violence/)).

## Adopted references

- `8` [Ācārāṅga Sūtra](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/acaranga-sutra) (trans. Hermann Jacobi, Sacred Books of the East, 1884, on Wisdom Library): the first text of the Svetambara canon. Its first book is the classic statement of non-violence towards all living beings, and Book 2, Lecture 15 sets out the five great vows of monks and nuns.
- `8` [Tattvārtha Sūtra, with commentary](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/tattvartha-sutra-with-commentary) (trans. Vijay K. Jain, 2018, with Pujyapada's *Sarvarthasiddhi*, on Wisdom Library): the handbook of Jain doctrine accepted by both main traditions. Chapter 7 covers the vows of monks, nuns and householders.
- `7` [Vows](https://jainpedia.org/article/themes/principles/jain-beliefs/vows/) (Jainpedia, Institute of Jainology): the great vows of monks and nuns, the lesser vows and the twelve vows of lay people, confession and atonement, and the vow of a final fast.
- `7` [Jain Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/jain/) (Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy): Jain metaphysics, the doctrine that reality is many-sided (*anekanta*) and its logic, and the ethics of the five vows.

## Differences within the tradition

- `7` [Jainpedia, Digambara](https://jainpedia.org/article/themes/principles/sects/digambara/): **Digambaras and Svetambaras** differ over monastic practice, scripture and the spiritual status of women. Digambara monks go naked as a sign of complete renunciation; Digambara nuns wear white and so count technically as advanced lay women, and Digambaras hold that women cannot reach liberation. Svetambara monks and nuns wear white robes, and Svetambaras hold that women can reach liberation ([Jainpedia, Śvetāmbara](https://jainpedia.org/article/themes/principles/sects/svetambara/)). Each tradition has subsects, some of which reject the worship of images ([Jainpedia, Sects](https://jainpedia.org/article/themes/principles/sects/)).
- `7` [Jainpedia, Vows](https://jainpedia.org/article/themes/principles/jain-beliefs/vows/): **Ascetic and lay ethics differ in degree.** The great vows of monks and nuns are absolute and lifelong, and Jains believe that only those who take them can reach final liberation. Lay people keep lesser vows within family and working life and can move by eleven steps towards renunciation; the food rules they follow vary between sects, and many no longer observe all the traditional ones ([Jainpedia, Non-violence](https://jainpedia.org/article/themes/principles/jain-beliefs/non-violence/)).
- `7` [Tattvārtha Sūtra 7.22, with commentary](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/tattvartha-sutra-with-commentary/d/doc1084847.html): **The vow of a final fast (sallekhana)** lets a monk, nun or lay person, in particular circumstances near the end of life, give up food and the body with a calm mind. Jain teachers hold that it is not suicide, because it is undertaken freely and without passion, and the person who keeps it is greatly honoured ([Jainpedia, Vows](https://jainpedia.org/article/themes/principles/jain-beliefs/vows/)). In 2015 the Rajasthan High Court ruled the practice illegal, punishable under the Indian Penal Code's provisions on suicide and its abetment, and the Supreme Court of India stayed that ruling on petitions from Jain bodies ([Business Standard, SC stays Rajasthan HC decision declaring 'santhara' as illegal](https://www.business-standard.com/article/pti-stories/sc-stays-hc-decision-declaring-santhara-as-illegal-115083100305_1.html)).
- `7` [Jainpedia, Non-violence](https://jainpedia.org/article/themes/principles/jain-beliefs/non-violence/): **Modern interpretations** extend non-violence to activism for peace, the environment, animal welfare and the relief of poverty. The Anuvrat movement, founded in 1949 by Acharya Tulsi of the Svetambara Terapanth order, turned the lay vows into a set of moral vows open to followers of every religion ([Jainpedia, Aṇuvrat Movement](https://jainpedia.org/article/themes/principles/sects/non-sectarian-movements/anuvrat-movement/)).
