# Humanism

Humanism is a non-religious worldview, or "life stance": humanists base their ethics on reason, empathy and concern for human beings and other sentient animals, rather than on belief in God or the supernatural, and they hold that people can give their own lives meaning in this life ([Humanists UK, Humanism](https://humanists.uk/humanism/), [Humanists International, Humanism: FAQs](https://humanists.international/what-is-humanism/faqs/)). Many humanist organisations around the world are members of Humanists International, whose defining statement is the Amsterdam Declaration, first agreed in 1952 and revised in 2002 and 2022 ([Humanists International, The Amsterdam Declaration](https://humanists.international/what-is-humanism/the-amsterdam-declaration/)). National bodies include the American Humanist Association, which publishes the Humanist Manifestos of 1933, 1973 and 2003, and Humanists UK. Humanists see their values as part of long traditions of thought about ethics without religion, which the 1973 manifesto traces from ancient China, Greece and Rome through the Renaissance and the Enlightenment ([AHA, Humanist Manifesto II](https://americanhumanist.org/humanism/humanist-manifesto-ii/)).

Humanism has no scripture, founder or central authority. Its statements are agreed by members and revised over time: the third Humanist Manifesto describes itself as "not what we must believe but a consensus of what we do believe" ([AHA, Humanist Manifesto III](https://americanhumanist.org/humanism/humanist-manifesto-iii/)). The Amsterdam Declaration grounds morality in human life itself: it is "inherent to the human condition, grounded in the ability of living things to suffer and flourish", and needs "no source outside of humanity" ([Humanists International, The Amsterdam Declaration](https://humanists.international/what-is-humanism/the-amsterdam-declaration/)).

## Adopted references

- `9` [The Amsterdam Declaration](https://humanists.international/what-is-humanism/the-amsterdam-declaration/) (Humanists International): the defining statement of world humanism, in the version agreed by the 2022 General Assembly in Glasgow: humanists strive to be ethical, to be rational and to find fulfilment, and offer an alternative to dogma. The page also gives the history of the 1952 and 2002 versions and translations into many languages.
- `8` [Humanist Manifesto III](https://americanhumanist.org/humanism/humanist-manifesto-iii/) (American Humanist Association, 2003): six short principles on knowledge, nature, ethical values, fulfilment, relationships and society, successor to the manifestos of 1933 and 1973.
- `7` [Being Good](https://humanists.uk/wp-content/uploads/Being-Good-pages.pdf) (Richard Norman, Humanists UK, 2018): a short guide to the humanist approach to ethics: shared human values, consistency, consequences, empathy and imagination, and its argument that religious authorities and texts can't do our moral thinking for us.
- `7` [Humanism: FAQs](https://humanists.international/what-is-humanism/faqs/) (Humanists International): how humanists think about right and wrong, the ethical principles they draw on, secularism, and their attitude to religion.
- `7` [The 10 Commitments](https://americanhumanist.org/humanism/values/) (American Humanist Association): ten humanist values, each with a one-line pledge, from altruism, critical thinking and empathy to responsibility and service.

## Core moral teachings

In the order of the Amsterdam Declaration: ethics, reason, fulfilment, and openness. *Related rules* are the general and domain rules the teaching matches.

## Differences within the tradition

- `7` [Humanists International, Humanism: FAQs](https://humanists.international/what-is-humanism/faqs/): **secular and religious humanism.** Humanists International uses "humanism" for a worldview that is non-religious, and notes that the term "secular humanism" arose to distinguish it from other humanisms. The first Humanist Manifesto (1933), whose signers included several Unitarian ministers, instead set out the theses of a "religious humanism", which regarded the universe as self-existing and not created and rejected any supernatural guarantee of human values ([AHA, Humanist Manifesto I](https://americanhumanist.org/humanism/humanist-manifesto-i/)). Today there are movements such as Humanistic Judaism, which treats Judaism as the culture, history and ethics of the Jewish people rather than a religion, and congregational humanism, which keeps the community practices of a church or synagogue ([AHA, Humanist Glossary](https://americanhumanist.org/humanism/humanist-glossary/)). Some people describe themselves as Christian or Jewish humanists; Humanists UK argues that such uses don't make two distinct forms of humanism, one religious and one not ([Understanding Humanism, Can you be religious and humanist?](https://understandinghumanism.org.uk/articles/can-you-be-religious-and-humanist/)).
- `7` [Humanists International, Humanism: FAQs](https://humanists.international/what-is-humanism/faqs/): **approaches to ethics.** Humanists agree that ethics rests on human reason and responsibility, but they "do not have to agree" on the best moral theory. Humanists International lists tools they may use, such as John Stuart Mill's harm principle, the golden rule, John Rawls's "veil of ignorance" and principles of equality, justice and human rights. Humanists UK's guide to ethics weighs the consequences of actions, and also describes values such as kindness, honesty and fairness as virtues, qualities of character in the tradition of Aristotle and David Hume ([Humanists UK, Being Good](https://humanists.uk/wp-content/uploads/Being-Good-pages.pdf)). The [moral theories](../general/moral-theories.md) page sets out these approaches.
- `7` [Humanists International, Humanism: FAQs](https://humanists.international/what-is-humanism/faqs/): **contested moral questions.** Humanist organisations tend to take liberal positions on questions such as assisted dying, abortion and LGBT+ rights, on grounds of personal autonomy and the harm principle, and the 1973 Humanist Manifesto supported a right to die with dignity, including euthanasia, and the rights to birth control, abortion and divorce ([AHA, Humanist Manifesto II](https://americanhumanist.org/humanism/humanist-manifesto-ii/)). Humanists International notes that "not every humanist will agree on every issue", and many religious people hold opposing views: some Jews, Christians and Muslims, for example, read the commandment "Thou shalt not kill" as forbidding abortion and assisted dying. Humanists UK's guide to ethics presents assisted dying as a question on which people weigh compassion for the person who is suffering against the value of life ([Humanists UK, Being Good](https://humanists.uk/wp-content/uploads/Being-Good-pages.pdf)).
- `7` [Humanists International, Humanism: FAQs](https://humanists.international/what-is-humanism/faqs/): **attitudes to religion.** Humanists International says that humanists disagree with the truth-claims of religions, but humanists differ in how much weight they give to that: some describe themselves as anti-religious or anti-theistic, while for others the question of God matters little. Humanists International rejects bigotry against religious communities, defends freedom of religion and belief for everyone and works with religious groups on shared aims, while campaigning against blasphemy and apostasy laws and harmful practices.
