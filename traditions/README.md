# Religious and philosophical traditions

What the major religious and philosophical traditions teach about how to live and how to treat others, each in its own terms. Many of the moral rules people live by come from these traditions, and where the traditions agree with each other, and with international law and professional codes, that agreement is part of the evidence for the [general guidelines](../general/README.md).

> Entries are sorted by score from 1 to 10 ([criteria](../RANKING.md)): 9–10 start here or fundamental, 6–8 recommended, 1–5 further reading or situational.

Each page sets out a tradition's core moral teachings, cites the tradition's own texts in reliable editions and scholarly reference works, shows where its schools differ, and maps each teaching to the general rules it matches. The pages describe; they don't judge any tradition's claims, rank traditions against each other, or adopt one as the standard for the others ([ADR-0007](../docs/decisions/0007-describe-traditions-on-their-own-terms.md)). A short page can't do justice to a living tradition, so each page links to fuller treatments.

## Shared ground

The Parliament of the World's Religions' *Towards a Global Ethic* starts from a principle "found and has persisted in many religious and ethical traditions of humankind for thousands of years": *what you do not wish done to yourself, do not do to others*, or in positive terms, *what you wish done to yourself, do to others*. It calls this "the irrevocable, unconditional norm for all areas of life" ([Towards a Global Ethic](https://www.weltethos.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/Global-Ethic-Declaration_EN.pdf)). The tradition pages give each tradition's own form of this Golden Rule.

From it, the declaration draws five commitments, each of which matches general guidelines in this repository:

## Traditions

- [Judaism](judaism.md): the commandments (mitzvot) of the Torah and their interpretation in rabbinic law and ethics.
- [Christianity](christianity.md): the love of God and neighbour, the Ten Commandments and the teaching of Jesus, as the main churches read them.
- [Islam](islam.md): the Qur'an and the example of the Prophet Muhammad (the Sunnah), and the aims of Islamic law.
- [Bahá'í Faith](bahai.md): the teachings of Bahá'u'lláh on the oneness of humanity, justice, truthfulness and service.
- [Hinduism](hinduism.md): dharma, the restraints and observances (yamas and niyamas), non-violence and selfless action.
- [Buddhism](buddhism.md): the Five Precepts, the Noble Eightfold Path and the cultivation of compassion and loving-kindness.
- [Jainism](jainism.md): non-violence (ahimsa) above all, and the vows of truthfulness, non-stealing, chastity and non-possession.
- [Sikhism](sikhism.md): remembering God, honest work and sharing with others, equality and service (seva).
- [Confucianism](confucianism.md): humaneness (ren), propriety (li), righteousness (yi), filial piety and reciprocity (shu).
- [Daoism](daoism.md): living in accord with the Dao: simplicity, humility, compassion and non-coercive action (wu wei).
- [Humanism](humanism.md): ethics without religious belief, grounded in reason, compassion and human dignity.

## Open questions

- `7` [Religion and Morality in Western Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/religion-morality/) (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy): does morality depend on God? Divine command theorists hold that God's commands make actions right or wrong; their critics, going back to Plato's *Euthyphro*, ask whether God commands what is right because it is right, and many religious and secular thinkers hold that people can know right from wrong by reason and conscience.
