# Bahá'í Faith

The Bahá'í Faith arose in 19th-century Iran. Bahá'ís regard its founder, Bahá'u'lláh (1817–1892), as the most recent of the messengers of God, after the Báb. Bahá'u'lláh appointed his son 'Abdu'l-Bahá as the interpreter of his teachings, 'Abdu'l-Bahá appointed his grandson Shoghi Effendi as Guardian of the faith with authority to interpret them, and since 1963 the elected Universal House of Justice has guided the worldwide community, which has no clergy ([Worldmark Encyclopedia of Religious Practices, Bahá'í Faith](https://www.encyclopedia.com/religion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/bahai-faith-0), [Bahá'u'lláh and His Covenant](https://www.bahai.org/beliefs/bahaullah-covenant)).

Bahá'í moral teaching rests on the writings of Bahá'u'lláh and on the oneness of humankind, which Bahá'ís call "the pivot round which all the teachings of Bahá'u'lláh revolve" ([One Human Family](https://www.bahai.org/beliefs/essential-relationships/one-human-family)). It joins personal virtue, such as truthfulness, trustworthiness and chastity, with social principles: the elimination of prejudice, the equality of women and men, the harmony of science and religion, universal education, justice and peace ([What Bahá'ís Believe](https://www.bahai.org/beliefs)). The moral life is understood as the development of spiritual qualities through prayer, reflection and daily effort, particularly in service to humanity ([Character and Conduct](https://www.bahai.org/beliefs/life-spirit/character-conduct)).

## Differences within the tradition

- `7` [The Kitáb-i-Aqdas, Introduction](https://www.bahai.org/library/authoritative-texts/bahaullah/kitab-i-aqdas/3#981811850) and [The Universal House of Justice: A Unique Institution](https://www.bahai.org/the-universal-house-of-justice/unique-institution): Bahá'ís hold that authority to interpret the writings passed from Bahá'u'lláh to 'Abdu'l-Bahá and then to Shoghi Effendi, and that the **Universal House of Justice** may legislate on matters the writings do not explicitly address, but may not change laws they lay down ([Kitáb-i-Aqdas, Introduction](https://www.bahai.org/library/authoritative-texts/bahaullah/kitab-i-aqdas/3#455239592)). Bahá'ís regard obeying its decisions as a duty; individuals who disagree with a position are free to hold their views privately and to raise them with the Universal House of Justice ([Worldmark Encyclopedia of Religious Practices, Bahá'í Faith](https://www.encyclopedia.com/religion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/bahai-faith-0)).
- `7` [The Kitáb-i-Aqdas, Introduction](https://www.bahai.org/library/authoritative-texts/bahaullah/kitab-i-aqdas/3#811365653): Bahá'í law is applied **progressively**. Some laws are binding on all Bahá'ís now, wherever they don't conflict with civil law; others were given for a future society, and the community will be told when further laws become binding.
- `7` [The Kitáb-i-Aqdas, note 80](https://www.bahai.org/library/authoritative-texts/bahaullah/kitab-i-aqdas/13#532859618) and [note 134](https://www.bahai.org/library/authoritative-texts/bahaullah/kitab-i-aqdas/15#780366665): some teachings differ from modern human rights norms. Membership of the Universal House of Justice is confined to men, while women and men are equally eligible for national and local assemblies, and 'Abdu'l-Bahá is quoted as saying that the equality of women and men has been announced "except in some negligible instances" ([Kitáb-i-Aqdas, Introduction](https://www.bahai.org/library/authoritative-texts/bahaullah/kitab-i-aqdas/3#774789772)). Bahá'í law permits sexual relations only between a husband and wife, and Shoghi Effendi interpreted a verse of the Kitáb-i-Aqdas as prohibiting all homosexual relations.
- `7` [Worldmark Encyclopedia of Religious Practices, Bahá'í Faith](https://www.encyclopedia.com/religion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/bahai-faith-0): other perspectives are few. Some Bahá'ís have debated women's ineligibility for the Universal House of Justice, the teaching on homosexuality and the review of publications about the faith, and a few have left the community over such differences. Small groups that dispute the succession after Shoghi Effendi, such as the Orthodox Bahá'ís, each number around a hundred members. In rare cases where someone actively opposes the head of the faith and tries to set up an alternative version of it, the head of the faith may, after efforts at reconciliation, declare them a "covenant breaker", and Bahá'ís are not to associate with them.
