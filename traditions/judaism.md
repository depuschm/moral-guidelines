# Judaism

Judaism is the religion and way of life of the Jewish people. Its foundational text is the Torah, the first five books of the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh), which Orthodox Judaism holds was given by God at Mount Sinai. The rabbinic tradition, or Oral Torah, interprets it: the Mishnah (codified around 200 CE), the Talmud, codes of law such as Maimonides' Mishneh Torah, and a continuing literature of rabbis' answers to questions of Jewish law (responsa) ([My Jewish Learning, Orthodox Judaism](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/orthodox-judaism/), [My Jewish Learning, Tikkun Olam](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/tikkun-olam-repairing-the-world/)).

Jewish tradition has no separate discipline of ethics: its moral teaching is embedded in Jewish law (halakha), in theology and commentary, and in collections of moral sayings such as Pirkei Avot ([SAET, Jewish Ethics](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Judaism/JewishEthics)). It is grounded in the commandments (mitzvot) of the covenant between God and Israel, and in the teaching that every human being is created in the image of God ([Genesis 1:27](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.1.27)). The rabbis distinguish duties "between a person and God" from duties "between a person and another" ([Mishnah Yoma 8:9](https://www.sefaria.org/Mishnah_Yoma.8.9)), and they urge people to act "beyond the line of the law" where the strict law falls short of what is right ([SAET, Jewish Ethics: Jewish ethics and Jewish Law](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Judaism/JewishEthics#section3)).

Jews today belong to several religious movements, which differ on how binding traditional Jewish law is and how it may change, as the last section shows.

## Adopted references

- `9` [Tanakh](https://www.sefaria.org/texts/Tanakh) (Sefaria): the Hebrew Bible in Hebrew and English, by default in the JPS Tanakh gender-sensitive translation. The Torah holds the commandments, including the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20 and Deuteronomy 5) and the Holiness Code of Leviticus 19.
- `8` [Pirkei Avot](https://www.sefaria.org/Pirkei_Avot) (Sefaria): the Mishnah's tractate of moral sayings ("Ethics of the Fathers"), the only tractate with almost no laws; the classic short text of rabbinic ethics.
- `8` [Mishneh Torah](https://www.sefaria.org/texts/Halakhah/Mishneh%20Torah) (Maimonides, 12th century, Sefaria): one of the most influential codes of Jewish law. Its laws of Human Dispositions, Repentance and Gifts to the Poor set out duties of character, speech, repentance and charity.
- `8` [Jewish Ethics](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Judaism/JewishEthics) (Eugene Korn, St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology): how ethics is embedded in Jewish law, theology and commentary, its core values such as the image of God and imitating God's kindness, the musar movement, and current debates.

## Differences within the tradition

- `7` [SAET, Jewish Ethics: Positivist imperatives](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Judaism/JewishEthics#section1.1) and [My Jewish Learning, Orthodox Judaism](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/orthodox-judaism/): **Orthodox Judaism** holds that the Torah was revealed at Sinai and that the rabbinic tradition faithfully explains divine rules for living that are obligatory on all Jews today; as in traditional Jewish ethics generally, their interpretation is given over to human judgement and reason. It ranges from ultra-Orthodox (Haredi) communities, the most stringent in observing Jewish law, to Modern Orthodoxy, which seeks to harmonise traditional observance with modern life.
- `7` [Rabbinical Assembly, Committee on Jewish Law and Standards](https://www.rabbinicalassembly.org/jewish-law/committee-jewish-law-and-standards) and [My Jewish Learning, Conservative Judaism](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/conservative-judaism-how-the-middle-became-a-movement/): **Conservative (Masorti) Judaism** holds that Jewish law remains binding but can be adapted to modern realities. The responsa its law committee approves are the movement's official positions, but each congregation's rabbi may decide otherwise, and the movement accepts that opposing rulings on the same question can both be legitimate.
- `7` [CCAR, A Statement of Principles for Reform Judaism](https://www.ccarnet.org/rabbinic-voice/platforms/article-statement-principles-reform-judaism/) (1999) and [My Jewish Learning, Reform Judaism](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/reform-judaism/): **Reform Judaism**'s 1885 Pittsburgh Platform held only Judaism's moral laws to be binding. Its 1999 statement commits Reform Jews to study "the whole array of mitzvot" and to fulfil those that address them, and puts justice, tzedakah and tikkun olam at the centre. Classical Reconstructionist thought, following Mordecai Kaplan, sees the Torah as part of a civilisation that the Jewish people created and that continues to evolve ([My Jewish Learning, Reconstructionist Judaism](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/reconstructionist-judaism-and-the-rejection-of-chosen-people/)).
- `7` [SAET, Jewish Ethics: Present and future challenges](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Judaism/JewishEthics#section6): the movements differ sharply on the **roles of women and on same-sex relationships**. Reform Judaism ordained its first woman rabbi in 1972 and the Conservative movement in 1985, and in 2006 the Conservative law committee accepted a responsum that welcomed gay and lesbian Jews and opened the way to same-sex marriage. Reform and Reconstructionist Judaism removed traditional inequalities for women and same-sex couples, while many Orthodox authorities reject egalitarianism and keep the traditional prohibition of homosexual acts; some Modern Orthodox groups have begun to recognise women in religious leadership.
