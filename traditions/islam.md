# Islam

Islam is the religion of Muslims, who hold that the Qur'an is the word of God revealed to the Prophet Muhammad. Muslims follow the Qur'an and the Sunnah, the example of the Prophet as recorded in the hadith, and Islamic law (sharia) was developed from both by jurists of several schools ([SAET, Ethics in Islam](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Islam/Ethics), [The Amman Message](https://ammanmessage.com/the-three-points-of-the-amman-message-v-1/)).

> Entries are sorted by score from 1 to 10 ([criteria](../RANKING.md)): 9–10 start here or fundamental, 6–8 recommended, 1–5 further reading or situational.

The Qur'an is not mainly a book of law: only about 500 of its more than 6,000 verses deal with rules, and it offers general guidance for a godly life, centred on God-consciousness (*taqwa*), justice and mercy. Muslim ethical thought holds up God's qualities, such as justice, generosity and forgiveness, as a pattern for human character, and the Prophet as its model ([SAET, Ethics in Islam: Ethics in the Qur'an](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Islam/Ethics#section2)). Islamic jurists also teach that the law serves higher objectives (*maqasid al-shari'a*), the protection of people's essential interests ([Kamali, Maqasid al-Shari'ah Made Simple](https://iiit.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/MaqasidAl-ShariahMadeSimple-UPDATED-RED.pdf)).

Muslims differ in law, theology and spirituality: between the Sunni and Shia schools of law, between theological schools, between Sufi and other approaches, and between traditional and reformist readings, as the last section shows.

## Differences within the tradition

- `8` [The Amman Message, three points](https://ammanmessage.com/the-three-points-of-the-amman-message-v-1/): this statement, based on fatwas from senior scholars of all the schools and adopted by Islamic bodies from 2005 ([The Amman Message](https://ammanmessage.com/)), recognises eight schools of Islamic law as Muslim: the four **Sunni** schools (Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i and Hanbali), the two **Shia** schools (Ja'fari and Zaydi), and the Ibadi and Zahiri schools. It holds that they agree on the principles of the religion and differ only on its branches, and that such disagreement "is a mercy".
- `7` [Encyclopaedia Iranica, Ejtehād](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/ejtehad/): in **Twelver Shia** jurisprudence, qualified jurists (*mujtahids*) derive rulings by independent legal reasoning (*ijtihad*), and other believers follow a living jurist (*marja'*), generally the most learned of their day, on matters of law (*taqlid*).
- `7` [SAET, Ethics in Islam: The ontology and epistemology of ethics](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Islam/Ethics#section4): theologians disagree on whether reason can know good and evil without revelation. The Mu'tazilites held that it can and that good is objective; the Ash'arites held that good is what God commands and is known through revelation; the Maturidis took a middle path, with objective values that reason can know only dimly without prophecy. Shia theology adopted many Mu'tazilite ideas.
- `7` [SAET, Ethics in Islam: Sufism and the ethics of interiority](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Islam/Ethics#section3): **Sufism** developed a "science of hearts" that judges an action by the state of the heart in which it is done, with sincerity, purification of the soul and the emulation of the Prophet at its centre. Atif Khalil notes that the most influential reflections on ethics in the Islamic tradition are found in Sufi manuals.
- `6` [Pew Research Center, The World's Muslims: Beliefs About Sharia](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2013/04/30/the-worlds-muslims-religion-politics-society-beliefs-about-sharia/) (2013) and [Kamali, Maqasid al-Shari'ah Made Simple](https://iiit.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/MaqasidAl-ShariahMadeSimple-UPDATED-RED.pdf): **traditional and reformist** readings differ on how sharia applies today. Muslims differ widely on whether sharia has a single true interpretation or is open to several. Some hadith state or imply a death penalty for leaving Islam, which conflicts with the freedom to change one's religion in international human rights law (`RIGHTS-4`); in Pew's 2013 survey, support for that penalty among Muslims who wanted sharia as the law of the land ranged from 86% in Egypt to 4% in Kazakhstan. Other contemporary scholars read the law through its higher objectives, which some now extend to freedom and human dignity.
