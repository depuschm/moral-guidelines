# Confucianism

Confucianism (in Chinese, *Rujia*) traces itself to Confucius (Kongzi, traditionally 551–479 BCE) and to the sayings of Confucius and his disciples collected in the *Analects* (*Lunyu*). It has deeply influenced Chinese, Korean, Japanese and Vietnamese thought and society ([IEP, Confucius](https://iep.utm.edu/confucius/), [SEP, Confucius](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/confucius/)). Its central texts are the Four Books: the *Analects*; the *Mencius*, the teachings of Mencius (fourth century BCE), the "Second Sage"; and two short texts, the *Great Learning* and the *Doctrine of the Mean*. The Neo-Confucian Zhu Xi (1130–1200) grouped the four together, and they became the basis of the imperial civil service examinations ([SEP, Chinese Ethics: Neo-Confucian theories](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-chinese/#NeoConTheMorTheGroCos)).

Confucian ethics is an ethics of character and relationships. It asks people to cultivate virtues, above all humaneness (*ren*), through learning, ritual and the relationships of family and society, and to extend the care they learn in the family to others. The *Analects* presents Heaven (*Tian*) as aligned with the moral order but depending on people to realise it, and Mencius grounds the virtues in human nature, which he holds to be good ([IEP, Confucius](https://iep.utm.edu/confucius/), [SEP, Mencius](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mencius/)). The tradition has no single authority: its teaching has come down through many schools and commentaries.

Quotations below are from A. Charles Muller's translations, which give the Chinese text alongside the English. Translators render the key terms differently: Muller, for example, often translates *yi* as "fairness" where others write "righteousness".

## Core moral teachings

In the order the tradition itself presents them: first the five virtues that by the Han period were treated as a complete set ([SEP, Confucius: Virtues and Character Formation](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/confucius/#VirtCharForm)), then the relationships and practices through which they are lived. *Related rules* are the general and domain rules the teaching matches.

## Differences within the tradition

- `7` [SEP, Chinese Ethics: Xunzi versus Mencius on human nature](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-chinese/#XunVerMenHumNatOriMor): **human nature.** Xunzi (third century BCE), the third great classical Confucian, argues against Mencius that human nature is bad: left to themselves, people's desires lead to strife, and goodness comes from deliberate effort, teachers and the rituals the sages created, as a crooked board is steamed straight. Both hold that people must work to become good; they differ on whether that work grows what is in human nature or reshapes it ([SEP, Xunzi: Human Nature](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/xunzi/#HumaNatuXing)).
- `7` [SEP, Chinese Ethics: Neo-Confucian theories of morality](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-chinese/#NeoConTheMorTheGroCos): **Neo-Confucianism.** Zhu Xi grounded Confucian ethics in a metaphysics of principle or pattern (*li* 理) and vital energy (*qi*), and taught self-cultivation through study, investigating the patterns of things and respectful attention. Wang Yangming (1472–1529) objected that this was too scholastic: principle is in the mind itself, and the task is to remove the selfish desires that cloud the mind's original goodness. Both followed Mencius on human nature, and Xunzi was sidelined for centuries.
- `7` [SEP, Mencius: Later Influence and Interpretations](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mencius/#LateInflInte): **modern critiques and New Confucianism.** In the early twentieth century the May Fourth Movement called for China to Westernise and modernise rapidly, and Confucianism lost standing; under Mao Zedong it was dismissed as part of China's "feudal past". New Confucians in Hong Kong and Taiwan, many influenced by Mou Zongsan (1909–1995), restated Confucian ethics in dialogue with Kant. Since Mao's death interest has revived in mainland China, with debate on passages where Mencius seems to put loyalty to family above the welfare of the state.
- `7` [SEP, Chinese Ethics: Confucianism and gender](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-chinese/#ConfGen): **hierarchy and gender.** Critics hold that Confucian ethics supports patriarchal families and social hierarchy, which conflict with equality and autonomy: the classical texts place women in the domestic sphere, outside public office and the education that prepares for it, and filial piety was traditionally tied to a male family line. Some scholars argue for a Confucian ethics that fully recognises women's capabilities, or reformed in dialogue with feminist care ethics and democratic values; others question how much would then remain distinctively Confucian ([SEP, Chinese Ethics: Confucianism and liberal democracy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-chinese/#ConfandDemo)). A related debate concerns *Analects* 13.18, where Confucius says that a father and son should conceal each other's wrongdoing, which weighs family loyalty against public justice ([SEP, Confucius: The Family and the State](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/confucius/#FamiStat)).
