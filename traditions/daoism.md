# Daoism

Daoism (also written Taoism) is one of the three main currents of traditional Chinese thought. It takes its name from the Dao (道, "way"), and it includes both a philosophical tradition (*daojia*) and an organised religion (*daojiao*), which are deeply intertwined ([SEP, Laozi](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/laozi/)). Its classical texts are the *Daodejing* (*Laozi*), traditionally attributed to Laozi and thought to have taken shape between the sixth and third centuries BCE, and the *Zhuangzi*, associated with Zhuangzi (Zhuang Zhou, fourth century BCE) ([SEP, Chinese Ethics: Daoist ethics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-chinese/#DaoEth)). The earliest organised Daoist communities, such as the Way of the Celestial Masters, arose in the second century CE. Today its two main branches are the Way of Correct Unity (*Zhengyi*), heir of the Celestial Masters, whose married priests lead communal ritual, and the celibate monastic order of Complete Perfection (*Quanzhen*) ([SEP, Religious Daoism: Main Schools and Lineages](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/daoism-religion/#MainSchoLine)).

Daoist ethics grounds conduct in the Dao, the way of things, and in naturalness (*ziran*, "so of itself"). Rather than a code of rules, the classical texts recommend a soft, yielding, non-coercive style of action, few desires, and humility, and they warn that striving to be good can produce the opposite ([SEP, Chinese Ethics: Daoist ethics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-chinese/#DaoEth), [SEP, Laozi: Naturalness and Nonaction](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/laozi/#NatNon)). Religious Daoism treats ethical and moral principles as the foundation of self-cultivation, and its communities keep sets of precepts ([SEP, Religious Daoism: Nourishing Life](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/daoism-religion/#NourLife), [Kohn, Cosmos and Community: introduction](https://threepinespress.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/COSMOS-Cont.pdf)). Scholars debate how far "philosophical" and "religious" Daoism can be separated at all (see "Differences within the tradition").

## Differences within the tradition

- `7` [IEP, Daoist Philosophy: Is Daoism a Philosophy or a Religion?](https://iep.utm.edu/daoismdaoist-philosophy/#H3): **philosophy and religion.** Western scholars long treated the *Daodejing* and *Zhuangzi* as a pure philosophy (*daojia*) and the later religion (*daojiao*) as a decline from it. Many scholars now hold that the early masters did not separate ethics, self-cultivation, ritual and advice to rulers, and that the distinction reflects Western categories more than Daoism itself; the Daoist Canon does not make it either ([SEP, Religious Daoism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/daoism-religion/)).
- `7` [SEP, Religious Daoism: Main Schools and Lineages](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/daoism-religion/#MainSchoLine): **schools and ways of life.** The Celestial Masters (today's Way of Correct Unity) saw illness as a result of moral faults and healed through confession and repentance; Correct Unity priests may marry and lead communal ritual, while Complete Perfection (*Quanzhen*) is a celibate monastic order. Daoist rules differ with the kind of community (lay, monastic or millenarian) and the level of ordination, and they draw on both traditional Chinese values and Buddhist precepts: the Numinous Treasure (*Lingbao*) school took Buddhism as a model for rebirth, karma and precepts ([Kohn, Cosmos and Community: introduction](https://threepinespress.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/COSMOS-Cont.pdf)).
- `7` [SEP, Chinese Ethics: Ethical perspectives from the Zhuangzi](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-chinese/#EthPerZhuSkeQueAttGraThiIncAcc): **the Zhuangzi and the Daodejing.** The *Daodejing* gives advice to rulers and pictures a simpler society to return to; the *Zhuangzi* is more sceptical about knowing what is right and more pessimistic about changing society, and warns idealists that lecturing rulers can end badly. Interpreters differ on whether its sceptical, relativist passages or its passages on attunement to "the grain of things" come first ([SEP, Zhuangzi: Relativism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/zhuangzi/#RelaItDepeH)).
- `7` [SEP, Chinese Ethics: Ethical perspectives drawn from the Daodejing](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-chinese/#EthPerDraDaoSofStyActSocPri): **rejecting moralism.** The *Daodejing* says that if people "get rid of 'humaneness' and abandon 'rightness'", they will return to filial piety and compassion ([Daodejing 19](http://www.acmuller.net/con-dao/daodejing.html#div-20)). Read alone, this sounds like a rejection of the Confucian virtues, but the text also prizes compassion (chapter 67), and many readers take it as a call to recover a natural goodness that striving for virtue obscures. Readers also differ on whether the "soft" style is always better, or a corrective to people's habit of valuing the hard.
