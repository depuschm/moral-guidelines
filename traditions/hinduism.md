# Hinduism

Hinduism is a very diverse family of religious and philosophical traditions that grew up in the Indian subcontinent. It has no single founder, no single scripture and no commonly agreed set of teachings, and scholars note that no single doctrine, moral theory included, is shared by all Hindus ([BBC Religions, Hinduism](https://www.bbc.co.uk/religion/religions/hinduism/), [IEP, Hindu Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/hindu-ph/)). It is the religion of most people in India and Nepal, and there are large Hindu communities in many other countries.

Most Hindu traditions give authority to the Vedas, which end with the Upanishads, and to a larger body of "remembered" texts (smriti): the epics, the Mahabharata (which contains the Bhagavad Gita) and the Ramayana, the Puranas and the treatises on dharma (dharmashastras). Systematic schools, such as Yoga and the several schools of Vedanta, devotional movements and living teachers read these texts in different ways, and accept their authority to different degrees ([IEP, Hindu Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/hindu-ph/)).

Moral teaching is grounded above all in dharma: the order that upholds the universe and society, and the duty, virtue and right conduct it asks of each person. Many Hindus hold that dharma was revealed in the Vedas. It is universal, but it also differs with a person's age, gender and social position. The law of karma, by which actions bear fruit in this life and later ones, and the goal of liberation (moksha) from the cycle of rebirth give this teaching its frame ([BBC Religions, Hindu concepts](https://www.bbc.co.uk/religion/religions/hinduism/concepts/concepts_1.shtml)).

## Differences within the tradition

- `8` [IEP, Hindu Philosophy: Vedānta](https://iep.utm.edu/hindu-ph/): **The Vedanta schools differ on the place of dharma.** Shankara's Advaita (non-dualism) holds that people should follow the Vedic duties while they still see themselves as separate selves, but that a serious seeker of liberation must in the end leave dharma behind; admirers see this as freeing seekers from caste duties, critics as setting morality aside for spiritual goals. Ramanuja's Vishishtadvaita insists that dharma is never to be abandoned and joins it with devotion (bhakti), and Madhva's Dvaita holds that liberation depends on God, who is pleased above all by devotion.
- `8` [IEP, Hindu Philosophy: Purāṇas](https://iep.utm.edu/hindu-ph/): **Devotional traditions put love of a personal God at the centre.** Many Hindus belong to traditions devoted to Vishnu (for example as Krishna) or to Shiva. The Bhagavata Purana, for example, teaches a devotee's ethic that joins the Gita's selfless action to devotion, while largely keeping the duties of class and stage of life; devotional movements often describe right action as eternal service to God ([BBC Religions, Hindu concepts: Dharma](https://www.bbc.co.uk/religion/religions/hinduism/concepts/concepts_1.shtml)).
- `8` [IEP, Hindu Philosophy: Varna and Dharmaśāstra](https://iep.utm.edu/hindu-ph/): **Caste is defended, relativised and rejected within the tradition.** The dharma treatises tie many duties to class (varna) and stage of life, and critics note that they give Brahmins a privileged place; a Rig Veda hymn presents the classes as divinely ordained. Other strands relativise caste: the Yoga Sutra's restraints apply whatever one's caste rules, and Advaita thinkers deny that caste morality matters to a serious seeker. Modern reformers disagreed on how far to go: Gandhi worked to end untouchability but kept an ideal of varna as interdependent groups without enmity, while B. R. Ambedkar held that caste and untouchability could not be separated, judged the language of varna beyond repair and turned to a reconfigured Buddhism ([Barua, Revisiting the Gandhi–Ambedkar Debates over 'Caste'](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/items/0eb79425-9b96-46a9-b6ba-e94d58f25d89)). Caste, intermarriage and the position of women remain contested questions for many Hindus, including in the diaspora ([BBC Religions, Hindu history](https://www.bbc.co.uk/religion/religions/hinduism/history/history_1.shtml)).
- `8` [IEP, Hindu Philosophy: Classical Hindu Philosophy in the Context of Indian Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/hindu-ph/): **Non-violence and its limits.** The early Vedic rituals included animal sacrifice, which Buddhists and Jains criticised; vegetarianism, unknown in early Hindu practice, has since become part of Hindu orthodoxy in many parts of India. The Gita, in which Krishna urges the warrior Arjuna to fight a righteous war, is read as a contribution to just war thinking ([IEP, The Bhagavad Gītā](https://iep.utm.edu/bhagavad-gita/)), while Gandhi drew on ahimsa for his non-violent politics ([BBC Religions, Hindu history](https://www.bbc.co.uk/religion/religions/hinduism/history/history_1.shtml)).
