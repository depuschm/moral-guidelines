# Buddhism

Buddhism traces its teaching (the Dharma) to Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha or "awakened one", who was born in what is now Nepal more than 2,500 years ago ([BBC Religions, Buddhism at a glance](https://www.bbc.co.uk/religion/religions/buddhism/ataglance/glance.shtml)). Its living lineages fall into three broad traditions: Theravada, the main form of Buddhism in Sri Lanka, Thailand, Cambodia, Myanmar and Laos; Mahayana, the main form in China, Japan, Korea and Vietnam; and Vajrayana, practised in Tibet, Nepal, Bhutan, Mongolia and elsewhere in the Himalayas and Central Asia ([SEP, Ethics in Indian Buddhism: Forms of Buddhist Ethics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-indian-buddhism/#ForBudEth)).

The early discourses (suttas) of the Buddha are preserved in the Pali Canon of the Theravada and in parallel versions in Chinese, Sanskrit and other languages ([SuttaCentral](https://suttacentral.net/)); the Mahayana and Vajrayana traditions add their own sutras and treatises, such as those of Shantideva ([SEP, Ethics in Indian Buddhism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-indian-buddhism/)). Buddhist languages have no exact word for "ethics": the nearest, *sila*, means moral discipline, the settled commitment to a set of training rules. The most important of these are the Five Precepts for lay people and the much fuller rules for monks and nuns ([SEP, Ethics in Indian Buddhism: Basic Teachings](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-indian-buddhism/#BasTeaBudEth)).

Buddhist teaching grounds ethics in the path to freedom from suffering. Harmful actions hurt others and disturb the mind of the one who does them, and through karma they shape future lives; those who reach awakening are said to act from love and compassion for others. Buddhism does not centre on belief in a personal God ([SEP, Ethics in Indian Buddhism: Basic Teachings](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-indian-buddhism/#BasTeaBudEth), [BBC Religions, Buddhism at a glance](https://www.bbc.co.uk/religion/religions/buddhism/ataglance/glance.shtml)).

## Differences within the tradition

- `7` [SEP, Ethics in Indian Buddhism: Forms of Buddhist Ethics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-indian-buddhism/#ForBudEth): **Theravada** practitioners mostly aim to become *arahants* (saints) who are freed from rebirth, follow the Five Precepts as lay people and the full monastic code as monks and nuns, and practise the four immeasurables widely: the Discourse on Love is one of the most recited texts in Southeast Asia. Mahayana texts call this path "lesser" (*Hinayana*), a label the article describes as pejorative and its criticism as possibly unfair. Theravada monks, who live on alms, may eat meat given to them unless the animal was killed for them, so not all Buddhists are vegetarian ([SEP, Animals and the Environment](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-indian-buddhism/#AniEnv)).
- `7` [SEP, Ethics in Indian Buddhism: Beyond Moral Discipline](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-indian-buddhism/#BeyMorDis): **Mahayana** Buddhists take the bodhisattva vow to become Buddhas in order to help all beings, and organise the path around six perfections: generosity, moral discipline, patience, perseverance, meditation and wisdom ([Forms of Buddhist Ethics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-indian-buddhism/#ForBudEth)). Mahayana thinkers such as Asanga and Shantideva hold that an advanced practitioner moved by compassion may break the usual rules, for example lying to save someone from being killed, when that prevents more suffering; hatred, however, is never justified.
- `7` [SEP, Ethics in Indian Buddhism: Forms of Buddhist Ethics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-indian-buddhism/#ForBudEth): **Vajrayana**, the Buddhism of Tibet and the Himalayan region, shares the Mahayana commitment to become a Buddha for the benefit of all beings and can be seen as a branch of it; it differs mainly in ritual, imagery and meditation, using tantric means to reach Mahayana goals.
- `7` [Plum Village, The Fourteen Mindfulness Trainings](https://plumvillage.org/mindfulness/the-14-mindfulness-trainings): **Engaged Buddhism** applies Buddhist ethics to social and political life. The Vietnamese Zen teacher Thich Nhat Hanh coined the term for his 1964 book *Buddhism Entering into Society*, written for a country in turmoil and at war, and then trained young Buddhist social workers to rebuild bombed villages ([Plum Village, A Vision for Engaged Buddhism](https://plumvillage.org/books/1964-dao-phat-di-vao-cuoc-doi-buddhism-entering-into-society)). His Fourteen Mindfulness Trainings (1966) are a modern distillation of the Mahayana bodhisattva precepts, and his Five Mindfulness Trainings restate the Five Precepts for modern life, adding commitments to social justice, sharing with those in need, consent in sexual relations, not discriminating on grounds of gender identity or sexual orientation, and acting on climate change ([Plum Village, The Five Mindfulness Trainings](https://plumvillage.org/mindfulness/the-5-mindfulness-trainings)).
- `6` [Bhikkhu Anālayo, The Revival of the Bhikkhunī Order and the Decline of the Sāsana](https://www.buddhismuskunde.uni-hamburg.de/pdf/5-personen/analayo/revival-bhikkhuni.pdf) (Journal of Buddhist Ethics, 2013): **The full ordination of women** as nuns (*bhikkhunis*) disappeared from Sri Lanka around the eleventh century, and women renunciants in Theravada countries such as Thailand and Myanmar have lived without it. The order was re-established in Sri Lanka with an ordination at Bodhgaya in 1998, with the help of Chinese nuns, but some in the Theravada tradition reject these ordinations as invalid, partly because the Chinese nuns who took part follow the Mahayana. The author, a Buddhist monk and scholar, argues from the early texts that nuns help to prevent the decline of the Buddha's teaching rather than cause it.
