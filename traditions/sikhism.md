# Sikhism

Sikhism was founded in the Punjab, in what is now India and Pakistan, in the sixteenth century. It is based on the teachings of Guru Nanak and of the nine Gurus who followed him, and most of the world's Sikhs live in the Punjab, with large communities in other countries ([BBC Religions, Sikhism at a glance](https://www.bbc.co.uk/religion/religions/sikhism/ataglance/glance.shtml)).

The tenth Guru, Guru Gobind Singh, decreed that after his death the Sikhs' spiritual guide would be their scripture, the Guru Granth Sahib, which Sikhs honour as a living Guru; where it gives no answer, Sikhs decide as a community on the basis of its principles ([BBC Religions, Sikhism at a glance](https://www.bbc.co.uk/religion/religions/sikhism/ataglance/glance.shtml)). The Guru Granth Sahib contains the hymns of the Gurus and of other devotees (*bhagats*), such as Kabir ([Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1349](https://www.srigranth.org/servlet/gurbani.gurbani?Action=Page&Param=1349)). The *Sikh Rehat Maryada*, the code of conduct issued by the Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee (SGPC) at Amritsar, sets out how Sikhs should live, worship and keep the discipline of the Khalsa, the community of initiated Sikhs founded by Guru Gobind Singh in 1699 ([SGPC, Sikh Rehat Maryada](https://sgpc.net/sikh-rehat-maryada-in-english/), [BBC Religions, Amrit ceremony](https://www.bbc.co.uk/religion/religions/sikhism/ritesrituals/amrit.shtml)).

Sikh teaching grounds ethics in the One God, who is within every person. Everyone is therefore equal before God, and a good life is lived in the community, by living honestly and caring for others, rather than by withdrawing from the world or by empty ritual. Self-centred pride (*haumai*) separates people from God; liberation comes through God's grace ([BBC Religions, Sikh beliefs](https://www.bbc.co.uk/religion/religions/sikhism/beliefs/beliefs.shtml)).

## Differences within the tradition

- `9` [SGPC, Sikh Rehat Maryada, Articles I and XXIV](https://sgpc.net/sikh-rehat-maryada-in-english/): **Initiated (Khalsa) and other Sikhs.** The Rehat Maryada defines a Sikh as anyone who believes in the One God, the ten Gurus, the Guru Granth Sahib and the Gurus' teachings, and in the initiation (*amrit*) given by the tenth Guru, and who owes allegiance to no other religion. Those who take initiation join the Khalsa and accept a stricter discipline: they wear the five Ks, including uncut hair, avoid four transgressions (dishonouring the hair, eating meat of an animal slaughtered in the Muslim way, sexual relations outside marriage and using tobacco), give a tenth of their earnings and must be re-initiated after a transgression. The five Ks are worn by initiated Sikhs ([BBC Religions, The Five Ks](https://www.bbc.co.uk/religion/religions/sikhism/customs/fiveks.shtml)).
- `7` [Nirvikar Singh, The Three Pillars of Sikhism: A Note on Origins](https://sikhresearchjournal.org/index.php/srj/article/view/114) (Sikh Research Journal, 2019): **Modern summaries and scripture.** The familiar trio of remembering God, honest work and sharing (*nam japna, kirat karni, vand chhakna*) does not appear as a set in the Guru Granth Sahib. The article traces the formula to a 1907 novel by Bhai Vir Singh, whose chapter on it opens with Guru Nanak's line on working and giving (Ang 1245), and relates it to the scripture's own trio of the Name, charity and purity (*naam, daan, isnaan*).
- `6` [Pew Research Center, Attitudes about caste](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2021/06/29/attitudes-about-caste/) (2021): **Caste in practice.** The Gurus and the Rehat Maryada reject caste distinctions, but caste identities persist in Indian society across religions: nearly all Indians identify with a caste, whatever their religion, and about three-quarters of Sikhs in India (74%) say that all or most of their friends share their caste.
