# Moral theories

Scope: the main normative theories of ethics, which try to explain what makes actions right or wrong and a life good: consequentialism, deontology and Kantian ethics, virtue ethics, care ethics, contractualism and natural law. The file says what each theory asks you to pay attention to and how to use them together. The step-by-step decision process is in [Ethical reasoning and decisions](reasoning.md), the virtues themselves are in [Character and values](character.md), and religious ethics, including the religious roots of natural law, is on the [tradition pages](../traditions/README.md). Questions about what moral claims are at all, such as whether there are moral facts, belong to metaethics ([General guidelines](README.md)) and are listed below under "Open questions".

> Entries are sorted by score from 1 to 10 ([criteria](../RANKING.md)): 9–10 start here or fundamental, 6–8 recommended, 1–5 further reading or situational.

## Adopted references

- `8` [Consequentialism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/consequentialism/) (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy): the view that whether an act is right depends only on its consequences, from classic utilitarianism, which weighs the total good and harm for everyone affected, to rule-based and pluralist versions. It asks you to look at the outcomes of your choice for everyone affected.
- `8` [Deontological Ethics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-deontological/) (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy): theories on which some choices are required or forbidden whatever their consequences, such as duties not to kill, lie or break promises and rights that others may not override, and how they relate to consequentialism. They ask you to look at the duties, rights and limits that apply.
- `8` [Kant's Moral Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant-moral/) (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy): the most influential deontological theory, built on the Categorical Imperative: act only on principles you could will as universal laws, and treat humanity never merely as a means. Kant grounded the equal worth of every person in their capacity to govern themselves by reason.
- `8` [Virtue Ethics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-virtue/) (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy): the approach that puts character, the virtues and practical wisdom at the centre, going back to Plato and Aristotle in the West and to Confucius and Mencius in the East. It asks what a good person would do, and what your action would make of you.
- `8` [Contractualism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/contractualism/) (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy): T. M. Scanlon's theory that an act is wrong if it would be disallowed by principles that no one could reasonably reject, so that wrong acts are those that can't be justified to others. It asks whether you could justify your action to each person it affects.
- `8` [The Natural Law Tradition in Ethics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/natural-law-ethics/) (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy): theories, centred on Thomas Aquinas, that start from the principle that good is to be done and evil avoided, and from basic human goods that reason can recognise, such as life, knowledge and life in society. They ask whether an action respects or attacks those goods.
- `7` [Care Ethics](https://iep.utm.edu/care-ethics/) (Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy): the ethics of care, from the psychologist Carol Gilligan and the philosopher Nel Noddings onwards, which finds moral significance in relationships and in human dependency. It asks you to respond to the needs of particular people, especially those who depend on you or are vulnerable.

## Key rules

### Using several theories

- `7` `THEORY-1` **Look at an important decision through several theories, not only the one that comes most naturally to you: what it does to the wellbeing of everyone affected, which duties and rights are at stake, what it shows about your character, what the relationships involved need, and whether you could justify it to each person affected.** Each theory brings out features of a situation that the others can miss, and any plausible theory has something to say about consequences, rules and virtues alike. ([Markkula Center, A Framework for Ethical Decision Making](https://www.scu.edu/ethics/ethics-resources/a-framework-for-ethical-decision-making/), [SEP, Virtue Ethics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-virtue/))
- `7` `THEORY-2` **Treat agreement between the theories as a reason for confidence, and disagreement as a reason to look harder before you act.** When they point different ways, check the facts again, look for an option that meets more of their concerns, and talk the decision through with others (`REASON-12`). ([Markkula Center, A Framework for Ethical Decision Making](https://www.scu.edu/ethics/ethics-resources/a-framework-for-ethical-decision-making/), [Markkula Center, Thinking Ethically](https://www.scu.edu/ethics/ethics-resources/ethical-decision-making/thinking-ethically/))
- `7` `THEORY-3` **Be wary of reaching for whichever theory justifies what you already wanted to do.** People often choose the principle they apply on the basis of their feelings, their own interest or pressure from others, and switch between rule-based and outcome-based reasoning from one situation to the next. ([Ethics Unwrapped, Moral Reasoning](https://ethicsunwrapped.utexas.edu/glossary/moral-reasoning), [Ethics Unwrapped, Being Your Best Self, Part 2: Moral Decision Making](https://ethicsunwrapped.utexas.edu/video/best-self-part-2-moral-decision-making))
- `7` `THEORY-4` **Use the theories to inform your judgement, not to replace it.** None of them gives mechanical answers: even rule-based theories can't be applied well without practical wisdom, the ability to see which features of a particular situation matter most, which grows with experience. ([SEP, Virtue Ethics: Practical Wisdom](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-virtue/#PracWisd), [Markkula Center, Thinking Ethically](https://www.scu.edu/ethics/ethics-resources/ethical-decision-making/thinking-ethically/), [SEP, William David Ross](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/william-david-ross/))
- `7` `THEORY-5` **Test general principles against your considered judgements about clear cases, and those judgements against the principles, and revise whichever gives way.** This method, reflective equilibrium, is the main way moral philosophers check a theory; a considered judgement is one you make well informed, calm, and with nothing to gain or lose from the answer. ([SEP, Reflective Equilibrium](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/reflective-equilibrium/))

### A test the theories share

- `7` `THEORY-6` **Before you act, ask whether you would accept the action if you were in the other person's place, and whether you could justify it to everyone it affects.** The Golden Rule is found in many religious and ethical traditions, and Kant's test of willing your principle as a universal law and the contractualist test of justifying it to each person make the same demand: no special exceptions for yourself. ([Parliament of the World's Religions, Towards a Global Ethic](https://www.weltethos.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/Global-Ethic-Declaration_EN.pdf), [SEP, Kant's Moral Philosophy: The Formula of the Universal Law of Nature](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant-moral/#ForUniLawNat), [SEP, Contractualism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/contractualism/))

## How to apply

- `8` [A Framework for Ethical Decision Making](https://www.scu.edu/ethics/ethics-resources/a-framework-for-ethical-decision-making/) (Markkula Center for Applied Ethics): six ethical lenses, rights, justice, utilitarian, common good, virtue and care ethics, each with one question to ask of every option, inside a decision process. Supports `THEORY-1` and `THEORY-2`.

## Open questions

Questions on which serious traditions and thinkers disagree. These guidelines don't pick a side.

- `8` [Deontological Ethics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-deontological/) (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy): which normative theory is right, and whether a good enough outcome can justify crossing a moral limit, such as harming an innocent person to save others. Consequentialists judge acts by their outcomes alone; absolutist deontologists hold that some acts are forbidden whatever the outcome; threshold deontologists hold that limits give way only when the consequences of keeping them would be dire enough.
- `8` [Value Pluralism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/value-pluralism/) (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy): whether morality rests on one basic value or principle, as monists such as many utilitarians hold, or on several values or duties that can't be reduced to one another and must be weighed in each case, as W. D. Ross and other pluralists hold.
- `8` [Moral Realism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-realism/) and [Moral Anti-Realism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-anti-realism/) (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy): whether there are moral facts. Realists hold that moral claims can be true; non-cognitivists hold that they express attitudes rather than describe facts; error theorists hold that they try to describe facts but are all false.
- `8` [Moral Relativism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-relativism/) (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy): whether the truth or justification of moral judgements is relative to a culture or person. Relativists point to deep and widespread moral disagreement; many philosophers reject relativism, while some prominent ones defend forms of it.

## Tutorials and further reading

- `6` [Consequentialism](https://ethicsunwrapped.utexas.edu/glossary/consequentialism), [Deontology](https://ethicsunwrapped.utexas.edu/glossary/deontology) and [Virtue Ethics](https://ethicsunwrapped.utexas.edu/glossary/virtue-ethics) (Ethics Unwrapped, University of Texas at Austin): short explainers and videos on the three best-known theories, with examples of where each one struggles.
- `6` [Thinking Ethically](https://www.scu.edu/ethics/ethics-resources/ethical-decision-making/thinking-ethically/) (Markkula Center for Applied Ethics): a plain-language introduction to the utilitarian, rights, fairness, common good and virtue approaches, and the questions each one asks.
- `6` [Justice](https://justiceharvard.org/justicecourse/) (Michael Sandel, Harvard University): a free video lecture course on moral reasoning that tests utilitarianism, libertarianism, Kant and Aristotle against hard cases.
